Home Loan Brokers Brisbane Costs Explained

Broker payment in Australia generally falls into two buckets: commission paid by the lender after a loan settles, and fees charged to the borrower. ASIC Moneysmart's home loans guidance covers using a mortgage broker and comparing loan options across lenders, and it is the reference point borrowers can use to check what a broker should explain about how they are paid. Neither bucket has a fixed universal price, so the practical move is to ask each broker directly how they are paid, whether they charge an upfront or application fee, and whether any charge applies if a loan does not proceed.

The parent guide to home loan brokers brisbane covers how to shortlist by category and suburb. This page looks at the same decision from the money side, because fit and cost should be settled together before you commit to one brokerage conversation.

Fees Worth Asking About by Name

Instead of asking whether a broker is expensive in general, put four specific questions on the table:

  • Lender-paid commission. Ask which lenders pay commission, whether the rate you are offered is affected, and what the broker discloses about their payment.
  • Borrower-paid fees. Ask whether there is an upfront, application or service fee, when it becomes payable, and whether it is refundable if the loan falls over.
  • Switching costs. For refinancers, Moneysmart points borrowers to calculating the cost of switching home loans, so ask a refinance broker to set out exit costs and new establishment costs before recommending a move.
  • Lenders mortgage insurance. This is a loan cost rather than a broker fee, but it can be one of the largest extra items, and it typically comes into play when the deposit is small relative to the property value.

No fixed amounts for these items are published on the directory pages, which is exactly why the enquiry itself should include the money questions rather than treating fees as an afterthought.

Cost Questions by Borrower Scenario

The categories on the directory map neatly onto different cost questions:

  • First home buyers: ask what a smaller deposit does to the total loan cost, because lenders mortgage insurance is often the biggest extra item on a first purchase.
  • Refinancers: ask for a written comparison of switching costs against expected savings, which mirrors Moneysmart's switching guidance.
  • Investors: ask whether the loan will be priced and structured as investment borrowing from the outset. Moneysmart keeps property investment guidance separate from general home loan guidance, and the structure affects cost.
  • Construction borrowers: ask whether staged progress payments and non standard applications change the fee or commission arrangement.

Comparing Total Cost, Not Just the Headline Rate

A broker's value shows up in the total cost of the loan over its life, not only in whether their service is free to use. Moneysmart's guidance on comparing loan options across lenders, and on features such as offset accounts and interest-only loans, gives borrowers a checklist for that comparison. A broker who can explain features clearly, and who puts fee and commission details in writing, is easier to evaluate than one who leads with a rate alone. When two shortlisted options look similar, the second link in your research, comparing broker fit before you commit, is worth reading alongside the fee answers, because a cheap-looking loan with the wrong features usually costs more over time.

Who This Approach Applies To

This cost-focused approach suits borrowers who want the payment question settled early. It works for a first home buyer worried about upfront fees, a refinancer weighing switching costs against a possible saving, an investor checking that the loan is structured as investment borrowing from day one, and a construction borrower whose staged application changes the commercial arrangement. It matters less if you already have an established broker relationship and know exactly how they are paid, though a periodic fee check is still reasonable when rates or your circumstances move. Whichever group you fall into, keep the discipline from the parent guide: scenario first, then category, then suburb, then the money questions before you sign anything.

  1. Settle the objective first. Decide whether you are buying, refinancing, investing or building before comparing brokers, using Moneysmart's separate guides as a prompt where the purpose is unclear.
  2. Shortlist free of charge. Use the directory's categories and suburb filters to build a shortlist, and use the free quote requests to open contact with two or three brokers.
  3. Ask the payment question first. At first contact, ask each broker how they are paid, whether they charge borrower fees, and whether any fee applies if the loan does not settle.
  4. Compare total loan cost. Judge each option on rate, fees, lenders mortgage insurance and features together, not on the headline rate alone.
  5. Confirm details in writing. Before signing, ask for the fee and commission position in writing, and check the listing's advice to confirm fees and availability directly with the business.
